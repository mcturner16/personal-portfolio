Part 1 - Variations of the same Graph

Here I have a collection of my data analytics work which I will build and continue to add on to this Portfolio. Starting with some simple Vega-Lite practice.

This first chart takes a Movies Dataset from the Kaggle User SibamSamanta07 which contains metadata about 45,466 Movies. This chart looks at the median revenue in USD for each genre of Movies.

This second chart looks at the average revenue for each genre instead of the median. You can see how the rankings and the x-axis shifts.

For example, the median revenue for a Horror Movie in this dataset is $25,519,166, ranking 11th out of 20 Genres. Whereas the average revenue for a Horror Movie is more than double the median, at $53,896,875. However, this ranks only 16th out of the 20 Genres. Meanwhile the X-Axis roughly doubles in range compared to the first graph, but looks visually similar to the naked eye.

For the third chart, I decided to go back to the median but instead find the median "profit", defined as revenue minus budget.

You can see that Animation movies go from 6th in median and average revenue to 1st in median profit. Family movies go from 4th in median revenue and 5th in average revenue to 2nd in median profit. This is because of far lower budgets on average and median than the genres ranked above them in revenue - Adventure, Fantasy, Action and Science Fiction. Interestingly, Foreign and Western Movies dipped into negative median profit.

For the fourth chart, I naturally decided to look at the average profit.

The Adventure Genre reclaimed its spot as having the highest average profit, with Fantasy 2nd. Family and Animation dropped to 3rd and 4th, respectively. This is likely due to major blockbuster movies creating a sort of boom or bust dynamic. With the major hits dragging the average up, but the median staying lower due to less successful movies. The Adventure Genre's median profit is $54,269,486 and the average profit is $127,826,344.

Part 2 - Fixing a bad Visualization

Here we see a very misleading visualization published by the St. Louis Fed with the data sourced from the SIPRI Military Expenditure Database.

This post was made on January 22, 2023, showing the Top 6 Countries in the world by Military Spending. With the United States' budget being shown on the right Y-Axis, and the budgets of China, Russia, the United Kingdom, India and Saudi Arabia being shown on the left Y-Axis. This was obviously created as a scare tactic to make people look at it and think that China had surpassed the United States in Military Spending, when this is clearly not the case if you read closely.

With the data source taken directly from the SIPRI Database, I decided to update this graph to 2025, using 2024 constant dollars instead of 2020 constant. And I decided to stretch the timespan from 1990 to 2025. And as a result, with a single Y-Axis, the United States' Military Budget is shown to be far higher than 2nd Place China in reality to the naked eye. However, it does leave the other smaller countries at the bottom looking a bit cluttered.

Initially I decided to take the Top 10 Countries and aggregate the rest of the world into another category. However, I ultimately decided against this because having 10 lines made the bottom feel too cluttered, and the "Rest of World" Category would have itself been highly misleading because the dataset contained many null values that I had no way of knowing, and thus set to 0. For example - Do you really think that North Korea has a Military Budget of $0 in the year 2025? According to this graph, it would have, even if the underlying data was under the hood.

In order to better represent the differences in Military Spending of the other countries in the Top 6 without having two confusing Y-Axes, I decided to create a second graph excluding the United States itself, so that the Y-Axis can be shrunk down significantly.

Part 3 - Different Interpretations of the same Data.

Today, I will take a look at the adoption of Electric Vehicles in the United States, China and Worldwide. The data was compiled and sourced from the Ember Think Tank's free EV Sales Data Tracker.

The point of this exercise is to show how trends can be interpreted with long term and short term data.

This graph shows the percentage of vehicles sold that are Battery Electric or Plug-In Hybrid in each month beginning in January 2016. From this data you can see that the United States has undeniably become a laggard to the global trend, and that China (powered by its domestic car industry) has become a global leader in EV Adoption. While the United States is well below average in EV Adoption, possibly due to large ranges of car usage, lack of charging stations or cultural and political opposition - the long term graph still shows an upward trend despite the peak in September 2025 and then subsequent dip.

Note on Seasonality: The sharp, recurring dips visible in the graph every January and February in China are normal seasonal patterns due to tax breaks expiring yearly in December, combined with the Chinese New Year. You can read about this phenomenon being documented in the South China Morning Post.

If you shrink the scale to only beginning in January 2025 - You will see that the share of EVs being sold in the United States peaked in September 2025, and looks to be on a downward trend since then. This is due to the Trump Administration's decision to end the Electric Vehicle Tax Credit on September 30, 2025. If you look at this short term trend, you may come to the conclusion that the United States is a global laggard doomed to never mass adopt EVs, and rely on ICE (Internal Combustion Engine) Vehicles permanently. Indeed, the Big 3 Automakers posted a loss of over $52 Billion in write-downs, charges and cancelled supplier contracts. However, looking closely, you may note that the share of EVs has slowly begun its upward trend again in the United States from the nadir of November 2025.

I believe it is very easy to mislead readers with data visualizations by presenting the same data. As seen in this example where I showed the adoption of Electric Vehicles with the same dataset, while using different beginning points in order to show what look like different long term trends. And also seen in Part 2 of my work where I displayed an example of fixing a bad visualization.

Part 4 - Tutorial for how to use a Software.

QGIS is an open source software meant to be used for mapping and geographic data. Specifically using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) Data. It is particularly useful and popular for mapping Election Results, which I will demonstrate in this tutorial using data from the recent 2026 Midterm Elections in the United States.

My tutorial builds on Chris Kirkwood’s QGIS tutorial, which was created using a Precinct Map for the 2020 Presidential Election in Transylvania County, North Carolina. In this guide, I will be showing how to create a similar map for the 2026 US Senate Election in my Home County of Oakland County, Michigan. I will also share more general tips about how to collect spatial and election data necessary for QGIS Visualizations.

Step 1: Download QGIS

QGIS is free and open source, available on Windows, macOS, Linux, Android and iOS.

If you choose OSGeo4W Installer for Windows, like I chose, click “Express Install” which is not selected by default. As per the recommendations of QGIS Developers themselves. When the “Choose A Download Site” page appears, select the https://download.osgeo.org option and click next, as that is the official main server for QGIS.

When prompted to Select Packages – Select either QGIS or QGIS LTR. I personally chose QGIS as it is the newest regular release, and will be automatically updated with new features, and I intend to continue experimenting after the class is finished. Whereas QGIS LTR is a Long Term Release, and is the most stable version, which will work perfectly fine for beginners with little chance of running into unexpected bugs. Skip GDAL and GRASS GIS as these are unnecessary for elections mapping.

Note: If you choose the Windows Offline Installer or the macOS DMG Installer instead, you will only be prompted to choose between the latest release and LTR directly.

Step 2: Collecting Precinct Data

Votes in Elections are collected and counted at sites called Precincts. So in order to map them, we will need shapefiles for precincts in our specific County of choice. Chris Kirkwood’s tutorial recommended using precincts from the Census Bureau for the 2020 Election at this link:, but this recommendation is outdated. According to the US Census Bureau Redistricting Data Program, the Census Bureau only uploads Precinct Data once every 10 years during the decennial census, while collecting updates every 2 years only for congressional and state legislative districts. Because local precincts frequently split or consolidate between censuses, relying on 2020 files for a 2026 Election will result in outdated boundaries.

So how do we find the most up to date Precinct Data? I would recommend checking a State GIS Portal first, or a County GIS Portal if your State does not maintain a centralized statewide database. Not every State maintains a portal of updated GIS Data, leaving some to individual counties. But luckily, Michigan does. You can find Michigan’s portal here. On this website, search for “2026 Voting Precincts” (you can also find precinct files for other election cycles here). In my case, I filtered the dataset on County FIPS for “125”, which is the FIPS Code for Oakland County. Download both the CSV option and the Shapefile option, extracting it to a dedicated project folder where you can easily find them. I specifically want you to have access to a CSV (or Excel) file plus a .shp file.

Important Note for State Portals: On ArcGIS-based portals like Michigan's, clicking Download right away will give you the CSV and Shapefile for the entire state, which is way more data than you need for a single-county map. To avoid this, look for the Filter (or Toggle Filters) option on the dataset page before downloading. In my case, I filtered County FIPS to 125 (Oakland County) and made sure the Toggle Filters option was selected, so I only pulled Oakland County's precincts.

Note: Keep in mind that this is a beginner tutorial. Precinct Data on the County Level is not the only scale you can map in QGIS —you can also build maps at the State Legislative, Congressional District, Statewide, or even Nationwide level. Additionally, QGIS can be used to map Elections around the world, not just in the United States.

Step 3: Collecting Election Data

To access specific Precinct data for any US Election, there are Precinct by Precinct results found on the Secretary of State (or State Board of Elections) Website for each State. In the case of Michigan, the Secretary of State Website hosts Precinct by Precinct Election Results dating back to 1998. This can be seen as the final entry in the table hosted at the Michigan Department of State Election Results and Data page. However, the Precinct files for 2026 have not been uploaded to the site as of the date this tutorial was created, because results are yet to be certified and uploaded to the public archive.

Fortunately, there are easy workarounds to this problem. You can visit individual County Election Websites to find unofficial preliminary results right after Election Day, although note these will not be final numbers. In Oakland County’s case, the County Website gives a link to a portal where you can download an excel file with detailed results going by precinct, and you can click on Unofficial Election Results under the 2026 November Election tab. Step 4: Data Cleaning

If you have collected the spreadsheets I have described properly – You should have a spreadsheet representing the shape file from the Precinct Portal (2026_Voting_Precincts.csv in my case) from which the only utility you need is to find the unique identifier of each precinct which will be joined between the Shape File and the Results File. In this case, it is the PrecinctLongName Column, which needs to be aligned character for character. I personally like to transfer this to a new Excel Sheet which will serve as the Master Spreadsheet for the project, which will also be combined with the raw numbers from the precinct results file (details.xlsx in my case.)

details.xlsx contains multiple tabs, one for each race on the ballot, so I had to navigate to the U.S. Senator tab and then copied the data onto my new Master Spreadsheet. I added the Precinct Name Column and matched them to the Precinct Names from the Shapefile (VERY IMPORTANT: You must use the exact text of the names from the Shapefile, not the Results File.) You will notice that the raw export contains many unnecessary columns – such as separate breakdowns for Election Day, Absentee and Early Votes – whereas for the purposes of this tutorial, I only care about the Total Votes Column for each Candidate. I deleted the unnecessary columns (including third party Candidates who did not win any Precincts in Oakland County). I then re-labeled the Candidate Vote Total Columns clearly, adding Percentage Columns by dividing Candidate Numbers by the Total votes Column for all votes combined (using a standard spreadsheet formula like =B2/F2 that you can drag down across every row). And to make the eventual color scheme work with the pre-built New Partisan Rev style File from Chris Kirkwood’s Tutorial – I will be made a Republican minus Democratic Margin Column by subtracting the percentage for Mike Rogers in each Precinct by the percentage for Abdul El-Sayed in each Precinct, with the formula =E2-C2.

Note: There are 405 Precincts in the Election Results Spreadsheet compared to 404 in the Voting Precincts Shapefile Spreadsheet. This is because one Precinct – Fenton Precinct 2 – has the vast majority of its land area in neighboring Genesee County, with only a tiny sliver extending into Oakland County. For the purposes of the Project, I removed the row for Fenton Precinct 2 in the Master Spreadsheet so it would not appear as a piece of land jutting outside Oakland County’s borders on the map. However, in the final map layout where I manually type the summary header, I used the official vote totals for the entire county so those 15 votes are still accurately accounted for.

Step 5: QGIS

Now here is the actually fun part, given the old truism that 90% of data visualization is data cleaning.

Open QGIS Desktop and click New Project (the blank page icon at the top left). Drag and drop the .shp File from your dedicated folder into the big white map canvas in the middle. Then drag and drop your finished Master Data File (data.xlsx in my case) into the Layers panel on the bottom left, where it will appear as a little spreadsheet table icon.

Next, if necessary, you will need to fix the map projection if your county looks distorted. Don’t worry—this is natural. Click the EPSG globe icon in the very bottom-right corner of the QGIS window and find a filter covering the area where your county is located. You can tell your projection is already fine if the county's proportions look normal on screen and the bottom-right corner displays a local or statewide code (such as EPSG:3078 in Michigan) rather than EPSG:4326 (unprojected GPS coordinates) or Unknown CRS.

This third part of QGIS is optional. Whether you want to implement a street map underneath the precincts is up to you. If you do, click Plugins -> Manage and Install Plugins in the top menu bar. In the resulting popup window, search for “QuickMapServices” and click Install Plugin, then close the popup. Next, in the top menu bar, click Web -> QuickMapServices -> OSM -> OSM Standard. This will add an OSM Standard layer to the Layers panel. Put it as the top layer, right-click OSM Standard and go to Properties, set Transparency to 90% (or another number you can play around with), and under the Symbology tab set the Blending Mode menu to Multiply. All of those steps will result in your precincts overlaid cleanly over OpenStreetMap.

Now for the best part: joining and coloring the map. Right-click the Precinct layer in the Layers panel and click Properties. Go to the Joins tab and click the green + button at the bottom. Set Join Layer to your main spreadsheet layer and match your Join Field and Target Field on the unique identifiers (PrecinctLongName), then click OK. For the purpose of this tutorial, we can use a premade color scheme to shade the county. You can find this premade red-to-blue color File in Chris Kirkwood’s Google Drive—look for New Partisan Rev.qml, download it, and store it in your project folder. Back in QGIS, open Properties for the Precinct layer again and go to the Symbology tab. At the bottom left, click Style -> Load Style, select New Partisan Rev.qml (either directly in File Explorer or via the ... button depending on your QGIS version), and click Open / Load Style. Finally, make sure the Value dropdown at the top of the Symbology tab is set to your joined GOP Margin column, then click OK.

Finally, to make the map look more professional and presentable, create a Print Layout. Go to Project -> New Print Layout and name your layout in the popup. Click the Add Map icon on the left toolbar and drag a large rectangle across most of the white page so your map fills the canvas. Next, click the Add Legend icon and drag a box in an empty corner of the page. In the Item Properties panel on the right, expand Legend Items, change Synchronize to Visible Layers to Manual, and rename the precinct layer to “Precinct Margin” while renaming the OSM Standard layer to a single blank space so the legend looks clean. Finally, click the Add Label icon on the left toolbar to add a title and countywide results summary box, editing the text and font in the right-hand properties panel. Once everything looks right, click Layout -> Export as Image... in the top menu bar to save your finished map.
